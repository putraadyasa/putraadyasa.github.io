Key Findings

What the data reveals

📍
Eixample is the most populous district
With the highest population density among Barcelona's 10 districts, Eixample dominates both population counts and unemployment figures.
✈️
Spain, Italy, and Pakistan are top immigrant origins
In 2017, the largest immigrant groups came from within Spain (other provinces), followed by Italy, Pakistan, and China — reflecting both EU mobility and historical migration patterns.
👶
Most districts have negative natural growth
Deaths outnumber births in the majority of Barcelona's districts, with Eixample showing the most negative net natural growth in 2017.
♀️
Women live longer in every district
Life expectancy for women consistently exceeds men across all districts and all years in the dataset, with a gap of roughly 5–6 years on average.
📉
Unemployment peaked in winter months
Registered unemployment in 2017 was highest in January–February and lowest in June–September, consistent with seasonal employment patterns in a tourism-driven economy.

Does air quality affect life expectancy in Barcelona?

A district-level correlation analysis using Kaggle datasets — cleaning and aggregating data in Python, then testing Pearson correlations with scipy.

Author Putra Adyasa Jatnika
Tools Python · pandas · matplotlib · scipy
Data source Kaggle — xvivancos
Code GitHub  ·  Kaggle

Overview

The question

Air pollution is one of the leading environmental health risks in European cities. Barcelona, like many dense urban centres, has districts with measurable differences in pollutant levels. This project asks: do the districts with worse air quality also have lower life expectancy?

Using three pollutants — NO₂, O₃, and PM10 — measured across 10 Barcelona districts in November 2017, and life expectancy data from 2006–2014, I ran a Pearson correlation test to look for a statistical relationship.

Dataset

Six datasets, cleaned and joined

All data comes from the Barcelona Data Sets collection on Kaggle.

Air Quality
NO₂, O₃, PM10 readings by station · November 2017
Life Expectancy
Average by district · 2006–2014 rolling windows
Population
District population totals · 2013–2017

Methodology

From raw data to correlation test

Clean each dataset in Python
Handled missing values with dropna(), aggregated measurement stations by median, and normalised district names for joining across tables.
Aggregate pollutants per district
Computed average NO₂, O₃, and PM10 concentration per district. Joined with life expectancy data on district name. This produced a table of 10 rows — one per Barcelona district.
Test in Python with scipy
Ran scipy.stats.pearsonr() on each pollutant against average life expectancy. Generated scatter plots to visualise the relationship. Assessed statistical significance using p-value threshold of 0.05.
from scipy import stats
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv('/kaggle/input/datasets/putraadyasa/barcelona-analysis/barcelona_NO2.csv')

# Pearson correlation: NO2 vs life expectancy
r, p = stats.pearsonr(df['avg_NO2'], df['life_expectancy'])

print(f'NO2  →  r = {r:.3f},  p-value = {p:.3f}')
# Output: NO2  →  r = -0.232,  p-value = 0.617

Findings

No statistically significant correlation found

All three pollutants showed weak correlations and p-values well above the 0.05 significance threshold. The result is inconclusive — not a null result, but a data limitation result.

NO₂ · ~28 µg/m³ avg −0.232 p = 0.617 Not significant
O₃ · ~35 µg/m³ avg +0.223 p = 0.719 Not significant
PM10 · ~22 µg/m³ avg −0.171 p = 0.783 Not significant

A p-value above 0.05 means we cannot rule out that the correlation happened by chance. With only 10 data points and a time mismatch between datasets, these results cannot confirm or deny a real relationship between air quality and life expectancy in Barcelona.

Limitations

Why the result may not tell the full story

⏱
Time mismatch
Air quality data is a single snapshot from November 2017. Life expectancy is an average from rolling 5-year windows between 2006 and 2014. These time periods do not overlap.
📏
Small sample size
The analysis uses 10 data points — one per district. Statistical tests need larger samples to produce reliable results. 10 points is insufficient for Pearson correlation to be meaningful.
🔍
Missing confounders
Life expectancy is strongly influenced by income level, healthcare access, lifestyle, and housing quality — none of which are in this dataset. Air quality alone explains only a fraction of the variation.

Conclusion

What I learned

This project demonstrates a complete analytics workflow: raw open data → Python cleaning → statistical testing → honest reporting of results. The null result is a legitimate outcome — real analysis often produces inconclusive findings, and knowing why the data falls short is part of the work.

A stronger analysis would require multi-year air quality data matched to the same time period as the health data, neighborhood-level granularity, and additional socioeconomic variables. That is the next version of this project.
